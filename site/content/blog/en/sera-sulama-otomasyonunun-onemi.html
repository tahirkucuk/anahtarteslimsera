Irrigation automation is usually treated as a comfort item: "you don't have to open and close valves manually." That is the least important benefit. The real issue is how much the moisture level and salinity in the plant's root zone fluctuates throughout the day — and how directly that fluctuation affects yield.

The hidden cost of manual irrigation is variability

In a manually irrigated greenhouse, the day runs through three or four watering rounds. After each one, the root zone becomes saturated, then slowly dries out over the following hours. The plant oscillates between these two extremes. For root-sensitive crops like tomatoes, this swing causes two problems: calcium uptake disorders such as blossom-end rot, and a loss of uniformity in fruit size.

This is where automation intervenes. Splitting the day from three large irrigations into twelve small ones creates a much narrower moisture band with the same total volume of water. The energy the plant would have spent on adaptation goes into the fruit instead.

Key distinction Automation does not mean "less water" — it means "more precise water." The drop in the water bill is a side effect, not the goal. The goal is a stable root zone.

Automation without fertigation is half a system

Treating irrigation automation separately from fertilisation is a common mistake. In a modern greenhouse, water is a carrier; what it contains matters more than how much there is. The fertigation unit injects nutrient solution from tanks A and B, and pH correction from an acid tank, directly into the line.

Two values are continuously monitored here:

  • EC (electrical conductivity) — the total salt concentration of the solution. When it rises, the plant struggles to absorb water; when it drops, nutrients become insufficient.
  • pH — determines nutrient availability. When it falls outside range, the nutrients are present in the water but the plant cannot absorb them.

The control unit reads these two values in real time and adjusts the dosing pumps accordingly. Achieving the same precision with manual fertilisation is not possible — measurements happen once a day and corrections wait until the next.

Four areas where automation genuinely pays

1. Water and fertiliser

In sensor-driven irrigation, the drainage rate is kept within a target band. With manual irrigation, extra water is usually applied to stay on the safe side — and extra water carries extra fertiliser away with it.

2. Labour

Opening and closing valves, mixing tanks, and walking the sections takes up a significant part of a worker's day. When those hours are redirected to harvesting and plant care, the return becomes immediately visible.

3. Uniformity

Pressure-compensated drippers and correctly sized lateral lines ensure the plant at the start of the greenhouse receives the same water as the one at the end. This means the harvest can be collected within the same week — a detail that is not insignificant from a marketing standpoint.

4. Traceability

Automation keeps records. How much water was applied on a given day, how EC trended, where pressure dropped — all of this is visible retrospectively. When yield falls, you look at the log rather than guessing.

The situation differs for new builds and existing greenhouses

If you are building a new greenhouse, do not treat automation as something to add later. If irrigation pipe routes, suspension points, control panel location and cable ducts are established while the structure is still being drawn, cost goes down and installation time shortens. Systems added afterwards are fitted to a completed structure — more expensive and technically compromised.

If you already have an existing greenhouse, automation is often one of the quickest-returning investments you can make, because revenue already exists and the gain comes directly off operating costs. In that case, the starting point is a water analysis — filter selection and dripper type follow from it.

Questions to ask before deciding

  • Has the irrigation water been analysed? Suspended solids, iron and bicarbonate values determine filter selection.
  • How many zones will the system be divided into? Zone count is calculated together with pump capacity.
  • Is EC and pH measurement single-point, or is drainage measurement included?
  • Can the control panel be monitored remotely, and does it send alerts?
  • Is the service response time written into the contract?

The answers to these questions are not independent of greenhouse type and target crop. That is why we approach the irrigation project together with the crop plan and structural design — the three can each be individually correct and collectively wrong.
