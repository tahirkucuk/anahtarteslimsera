The most expensive mistakes in greenhouse investment are not made during construction. The steel is cut correctly, the glass is fitted properly, the irrigation does not leak — but the greenhouse has been built for the wrong crop, in the wrong location, or at the wrong scale. Every one of these mistakes could have been prevented in the decision phase that comes before construction.

The five topics below are the questions that must be answered before the steel is cut.

1. Crop and market feasibility

The sentence "let's build a greenhouse" is not an investment plan on its own. Which crop will be grown determines the greenhouse type, gutter height, heating capacity, and irrigation flow rate. A greenhouse built for tomatoes is not the same structure as one built for seedling production.

Feasibility answers these questions: At what price and in which season does the target crop sell? When does supply of this crop peak in your region? How much heating is required to produce out of season, and does the cost of that heating justify the price premium?

Common mistake Building the same greenhouse as the neighbour. Even within the same district, if land orientation, wind corridor, and water quality differ, the same structure will produce different results.

2. Soil, irrigation water, and climate analysis

These three analyses define the physical boundaries of the project.

  • Irrigation water analysis — directly determines the type of filtration station and dripper selection. If bicarbonate, iron, or suspended solids are elevated, the system is designed accordingly; correcting this afterwards is expensive.
  • Soil analysis — if soil-based production is planned, this is the starting point for the feeding programme. Switching to soilless cultivation changes the investment category and cost structure entirely.
  • Climate data — the region's snow and wind loads determine the structural calculation and therefore the steel tonnage. This directly affects one of the largest cost items in the project.

3. Scale and phasing plan

A question investors frequently ask is "how large should I build?" The answer looks at two things before the budget: the volume you can sell and the operation you can manage.

A greenhouse built too small spreads fixed costs (pump, panel, heating unit, staff) over too few square metres and drives up unit cost. A greenhouse built too large becomes unmanageable in the first season and yield falls.

The right approach is usually phasing: build the infrastructure (water source, pump capacity, main lines, electricity) to the target final size, but increase the covered area in stages. This prevents having to remove and replace infrastructure at the second phase.

4. Grant and subsidy eligibility

Grants and IPARD supports set their eligibility requirements before the investment decision, not after — they cannot be retrofitted. Land classification, investor status, and the call conditions of the current funding round determine eligibility.

One of the most common rejection reasons at this stage is inconsistency in the technical appendices: if the yield projections from the consultant, the capacity figures from the construction company, and the flow rates from the irrigation engineer do not confirm each other, the application is eliminated. For this reason, the grant file must be generated from the same technical data as the construction and irrigation specifications.

5. Operating plan and cash flow

When the greenhouse is built, the investment is not over — it is where the real spending begins. Seedlings, fertiliser, energy, labour, and post-harvest logistics all have to be paid before the first revenue arrives.

The operating plan reveals the first season's cash requirement. The most common difficulty investors face is a completed greenhouse with no working capital left. This is not a technical problem but a planning one — and it is visible at the feasibility stage.
