Irrigation system selection usually starts in the wrong place: the dripper brand. But the brand is the sixth decision. The correct order of decisions starts with the water itself, because the entire system is designed around what the water carries.

Step 1 — Irrigation water analysis

No irrigation project can be correctly sized without an analysis. The main parameters checked and what they determine:

  • Suspended solids — determines whether a sand filter is required. Almost always necessary for pond and surface water.
  • Iron and manganese — oxidise and block drippers. If elevated, aeration and pre-sedimentation are required.
  • Bicarbonate and pH — determines acid dosage and scale-formation risk.
  • EC and sodium — if salinity is high, a leaching allowance is added to the calculation; this directly increases total water requirement.
  • Biological load — algae and bacterial film are the most insidious cause of clogging.
Practical rule The great majority of dripper blockages are not product failures — they are filter selection failures. The filter is chosen based on dripper flow rate and orifice size, not the other way around.

Step 2 — Calculating water requirement

Daily water requirement is calculated from crop, growth stage, covered area, and the region's evapotranspiration values. The peak flow derived from this determines pump capacity and main line diameter.

The common mistake is calculating on average consumption. The system is sized not for the average but for the most intensive hour of the hottest day. Otherwise the system cannot keep up precisely when you need it most.

Step 3 — Dividing into zones

Irrigating the entire greenhouse simultaneously means oversizing the pump unnecessarily. The area is divided into zones and zones are irrigated in sequence. As the number of zones increases, the pump gets smaller but valve and automation costs rise; the optimum point is found by calculation.

A second advantage of zoning is the ability to apply different programmes to areas growing different crops or at different development stages.

Step 4 — Dripper type and spacing

There are two core decisions here:

Pressure-compensating or not?

If the land is sloped or the lateral lines are long, pressure-compensating (PC) drippers are essential. Otherwise the plant at the start of the line receives noticeably more water than the one at the end. Loss of uniformity means the crop matures at different times — a significant detail for scheduling harvests.

Flow rate and spacing

Dripper flow rate and inter-dripper spacing are selected based on soil texture. In sandy soil, water moves vertically; closer spacing is required. In clay soil, lateral spread is wider and spacing can open up. In soilless production, the decision is made entirely based on the water-holding capacity of the growing medium.

Step 5 — Fertigation unit

There are three ways to mix fertiliser into the water: manual tank mixing, venturi injection, and automated dosing-pump units. The first two are cheaper but cannot maintain stable EC and pH.

In an automatic unit, tank A (calcium-containing nutrients), tank B (phosphates and sulphates), and the acid tank are kept separate — this separation is chemically necessary to prevent precipitation, not arbitrary. The unit continuously reads EC and pH sensors in the line and adjusts dosing accordingly.

Step 6 — Automation and monitoring

The control panel can operate on a time programme, sensor-triggered irrigation, or a combination of both. Features to look for:

  • Independent zone-level programmes
  • Closed-loop EC and pH control
  • Drainage measurement (what fraction of applied water drains through)
  • Pressure-drop alarm — gives early warning of filter blockage
  • Remote access and historical logging

An automation system that keeps no records cannot help you when problems arise. The ability to look back is half the diagnosis.

The cost of breaking the sequence

These six steps are sequential. Choosing the dripper and then deciding on the filter, buying the pump and then determining the zone count — both approaches make the system start with compromises built in. The same logic applies to the greenhouse as a whole: the irrigation design should be ready while the structure is still being drawn, so that pipe routes and suspension points can be integrated into the building.
