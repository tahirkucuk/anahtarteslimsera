Your greenhouse irrigation schedule is set, your fertiliser doses are calculated, and your drip system is properly installed — yet plants are not growing as expected, and leaves show yellowing or burn marks. One of the most commonly overlooked causes is that the EC and pH values of your irrigation water are outside the target range.

EC and pH are the two fundamental measurements of greenhouse irrigation. Running a fertigation programme without monitoring these values is like flying blind: you may reach your target, or you may not — but you will never know why you missed it.

What Is EC and What Does It Mean for Crop Production?

EC (electrical conductivity) measures the water's ability to conduct electricity; the unit is mS/cm (millisiemens per centimetre). It indicates the concentration of dissolved mineral salts in the water — in other words, it is the indirect measure of the fertiliser concentration reaching the plant.

In greenhouse irrigation, EC is measured at two different points:

  • Mixed water (stock solution + source water): The EC of the solution leaving the fertigation unit and being delivered to the plant.
  • Drainage water: The EC of the water that has passed through the root zone and exits at drain points.

If drainage EC is noticeably higher than the feed EC, salt accumulation has begun in the root zone. This accumulation causes root damage and blocks the uptake of water and nutrients.

Target EC ranges differ by crop:

  • Tomato: 2.0–3.5 mS/cm
  • Cucumber: 1.5–2.5 mS/cm
  • Pepper: 2.0–3.0 mS/cm
  • Lettuce and leafy greens: 1.2–2.0 mS/cm

These values represent the combined EC of the irrigation water and the fertiliser solution. If your source water EC is high (>0.5 mS/cm), reduce the fertiliser dose accordingly.

Why Is pH So Critical?

pH indicates the acidity or alkalinity of a solution. The target range in greenhouse irrigation is 5.5–6.5. Deviating from this range directly changes the solubility of plant nutrients.

When pH rises above 7.0, iron, manganese, zinc and boron largely become insoluble — the plant cannot absorb these elements through its roots. Leaf yellowing (chlorosis) begins. What is often diagnosed as iron deficiency is actually inaccessibility caused by high pH: increasing the fertiliser dose makes the situation worse, not better.

When pH drops below 5.0, manganese and aluminium can reach toxic levels, causing root damage.

Key Priority: No matter how accurately you dose the fertiliser, if pH is outside the target range the plant cannot access those nutrients. The correct sequence is: bring pH into the 5.5–6.5 range first, then adjust EC to the target value.

How Are EC and pH Measured?

For small-scale greenhouses, a handheld EC/pH meter is sufficient, but daily measurements are essential. Points to pay attention to:

  1. Calibration: EC meters are typically calibrated with a 1.41 mS/cm standard solution; pH meters use 4.0 and 7.0 buffer solutions. Calibrate at least once a week.
  2. Temperature compensation: EC values change with temperature. Choose a device with automatic temperature compensation (ATC).
  3. Drain measurement: Measuring only the fertigation output is not enough. Always measure at the drainage point as well. Drainage EC reveals salt accumulation in the root zone.

pH Adjustment: Which Acid and How to Use It

Most water sources in Turkey have pH values between 7.0 and 8.5. Acid is used to bring irrigation water pH down to the 5.5–6.5 range. Common options include:

  • Phosphoric acid: Lowers pH while also serving as a phosphorus source. Can be preferred during periods of high phosphorus demand.
  • Nitric acid: Provides a nitrogen contribution. Suitable for periods with high nitrogen requirements.
  • Citric acid: Preferred in organic certified production; however, it is biologically degradable, so its buffering effect does not last long.
Caution: Acid dosing acts quickly. Start with small amounts and measure after each addition. Too much acid damages the plant and corrodes equipment.

Common Mistakes and Their Consequences

Frequent mistakes in EC and pH management include:

  • Increasing fertiliser without measuring: It is instinctive to raise the fertiliser dose when growth slows. But if root-zone EC is already elevated, this approach deepens the problem.
  • Measuring only the stock solution EC: The stock solution EC is extremely high and meaningless on its own. Always measure the diluted, ready-to-apply solution.
  • Setting pH once and forgetting it: Fertiliser type, seasonal temperature changes and source water fluctuations all affect pH. Regular measurement is essential.
  • Ignoring drainage: Not monitoring drainage EC allows undetected salt accumulation in the root zone to continue unchecked for extended periods.
  • Measuring at a single point: EC and pH can vary across different zones of a greenhouse, particularly in large structures. Establish multiple measurement points.
