Before starting a greenhouse project, you or your partners will inevitably ask the question: "Will this investment actually work?" A feasibility report is the technical-economic assessment that documents that question, backs it with numbers, and prepares it for submission to lenders or grant bodies. In this article we walk through what it contains and how it is prepared.

Why Is a Feasibility Report Necessary?

Some investors view a feasibility report as nothing more than a document prepared for a bank or grant application. That perspective is both misleading and costly. The data that goes into the report — land analysis, crop selection, climate system capacity, irrigation water quality, distance to market — are in fact the core decisions that define the project's architecture.

A well-prepared feasibility report serves four functions:

  • It prevents mistakes before the project begins, not after construction has started.
  • It shows under what conditions and in how many years the investment will pay back.
  • It allows you to speak the same language as a bank, partner, or grant body.
  • It anchors interim decisions — greenhouse type, heating system, production calendar — to concrete data.
Important: The hard part of a feasibility report is not writing it — it is putting the right data into it. A report filled with industry-average assumptions hides real uncertainty and misleads both you and the institution reviewing it.

What Sections Should the Report Include?

A standard greenhouse feasibility report contains eight sections. Each section must be filled with project-specific data; template sentences and generic tables may satisfy an institution but they do not serve the investor.

  1. Project summary: Greenhouse type, total area, location, target crop and production calendar.
  2. Land and infrastructure analysis: Plot size, slope, geotechnical survey, water source and power connection.
  3. Technical design recommendations: Structure type, covering material, heating and cooling system, irrigation and fertigation infrastructure.
  4. Cost analysis: Capital expenditure, annual operating cost and a line-by-line breakdown.
  5. Revenue projection: Estimated yield per decare, crop price analysis, seasonal plan.
  6. Financing and grant planning: Equity, bank loan and available grant options.
  7. Payback and profitability analysis: Net present value, payback period and scenario analyses.
  8. Risk analysis: Price risk, climate risk, production risk and mitigation measures.

Land and Climate Analysis: The Most Skipped Section

In practice, the weakest link in feasibility reports is the land and climate analysis. Investors typically start with land they already own or can afford, then fail to ask whether that land is actually suitable for greenhouse cultivation. Yet this section forms the physical foundation of the project.

Land analysis must answer the following questions:

  • Is the soil's load-bearing capacity adequate for a greenhouse structure? Are there landslide or flooding risks?
  • Does the irrigation water source — well, pond or mains supply — meet the project's scale in terms of flow rate and quality?
  • Do the plot orientation and wind conditions provide sufficient sunlight and heat retention throughout the day?
  • Is there an existing power connection, and is it three-phase? Is the capacity sufficient for irrigation pumps and heating?

For climate analysis, the last ten years of meteorological data for your region — minimum and maximum temperatures, frost days, sunshine hours, relative humidity — are essential for sizing the heating capacity. Without this data, the heating system will either be oversized, wasting capital, or undersized, causing crop losses.

When should irrigation water analysis be done? In many projects, water analysis is left until after the feasibility stage. This is a mistake. High chloride, bicarbonate or total salt content directly affects the irrigation infrastructure and fertiliser programme. The analysis should be done before the report is written, and its results reflected in the choice of heating and irrigation system.

Cost Analysis and Revenue Projection

The financial section of a feasibility report is built from two core tables: capital expenditure and a cash flow projection. Capital expenditure covers:

  • Land preparation and site infrastructure
  • Greenhouse structure and covering materials
  • Heating, ventilation and shading systems
  • Irrigation and fertigation infrastructure
  • Electrical installation and automation
  • First-crop operating expenses: seedlings, fertiliser, energy, labour

For the revenue projection, you need to compile wholesale prices for your target crop in your region over the past three years, along with average yield per decare. Rather than relying on a single price point, preparing three scenarios — low, mid and high — shows how resilient the project is under different market conditions. Avoid underestimating operating costs. Model energy, labour and depreciation realistically; otherwise the report presents a misleading picture to both you and the institution.

Financing and Grant Planning

Greenhouse projects typically draw on three financing sources: equity, bank loans and grant support. The feasibility report should link all three and present an optimal financing structure.

If you plan to apply for a grant, you need to subtract the expected grant amount from total project cost and recalculate your equity and loan requirements. Starting a project before grant approval significantly increases financial risk. On the other hand, waiting indefinitely for grant approval also creates opportunity cost — the report needs to make this trade-off explicit.

If a bank loan is involved, verify that the repayment schedule aligns with the cash flow projection. Especially in the first two years — before plants mature and yields stabilise — loan instalments can put real pressure on cash flow. Modelling cash flow on a monthly basis rather than annual averages gives a more reliable picture of that pressure.

Who Should Prepare the Report?

Two types of feasibility reports circulate in the market: template reports filled in for grant applications, and analytical reports that actually guide project decisions. Distinguishing between them matters.

A consultant or turnkey project firm with real sector experience can build the technical and financial sections in a way that is internally consistent. A consultant unfamiliar with greenhouse structures, irrigation and climate systems will fill the technical parameters with industry averages rather than project-specific modelling. The result looks good on paper but creates problems in practice.
