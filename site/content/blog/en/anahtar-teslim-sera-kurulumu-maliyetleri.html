Almost everyone who asks this question expects a single number: so much per square metre. The honest answer is this — a quote that gives a per-square-metre price either has hidden scope or will change later. A greenhouse is not a product; it is a project. Its cost depends on a series of decisions ranging from greenhouse type to site conditions.

Below we explain the five cost drivers and which decision changes each one. The aim is not to give you a number but to help you compare the quotes you have collected correctly.

The five cost drivers

1. Greenhouse type and gutter height

The biggest difference is here. The gap between Venlo glass greenhouse, polycarbonate, and double-layer plastic tunnel is not just the covering material — it is the entire load-bearing system. As gutter height increases, internal volume and climate stability improve, but steel tonnage and wind load also rise.

You can find the technical ranges for all three types in the comparison table on the services page.

2. Structural load — that is, the site itself

The region's snow load and wind load determine the structural calculation. Two greenhouses of the same dimensions, one in Antalya and one in Central Anatolia, will have significantly different steel tonnages. A soil investigation also changes the foundation type and therefore the volume of concrete.

When comparing quotes Ask which snow and wind load the structural calculation is based on. A structure calculated for a low load value looks cheap; you pay for it in the first hard winter.

3. Climate control level

Heating, ventilation, shading, fogging, and curtain systems are independent items and all of them are optional. If out-of-season production is your target, heating capacity becomes one of the main cost items. The choice of heating source (natural gas, coal, biomass, geothermal) changes both investment and operating costs.

4. Irrigation and automation

Its share of the total investment is relatively small, but it has the highest error margin: a single irrigation mistake can affect the entire crop. Options range from simple drip lines to fully automatic fertigation units with EC-pH control. Water quality determines the type of filtration station — an irrigation price given without analysis is an estimate.

5. Infrastructure and site works

Items that are invisible in most quotes but appear on the invoice: electrical transformer and panel, water source development (well, pond, reservoir), road and yard concrete, drainage, perimeter fence, storage and packing area. These are not included in the greenhouse itself but the greenhouse cannot operate without them.

Quote comparison checklist

If you have received quotes from three firms and the numbers vary significantly, the difference is almost always in scope. Ask these questions one by one:

  • What snow and wind load is the structural calculation based on?
  • What is the galvanisation thickness and profile cross-section?
  • What is the light transmittance and warranty period of the covering material?
  • What percentage of the covered area is ventilation opening?
  • Based on which water analysis was the irrigation price given?
  • Who is responsible for site preparation, foundations, and concrete?
  • Does the price include electrical infrastructure and panel?
  • Does it include freight and installation labour?
  • Does it include commissioning, testing, and training?
  • What is the quote validity period and which currency basis applies?

The last point matters especially. The share of imported equipment in greenhouse projects is high; a quote without a specified currency basis and validity period may convert into a different number by the time of signing.

The question that comes before cost: payback

What the investor is really asking is not the cost but the payback period. This is found by dividing the total investment by annual net cash flow — and the denominator is more uncertain than the numerator. Yield, sale price, energy cost, and operating expenses are all inside the calculation.

This is why an investment decision that skips the feasibility stage is essentially performing a division with an unknown denominator. We covered what needs to be done before building a greenhouse in a separate article.
