Greenhouse investment decisions usually start with the best intentions: there is land available, a crop idea has taken shape, and a few quotes have been collected. At this point, however, many critical questions remain unasked. Problems discovered after construction is complete are expensive and time-consuming to fix — they often require structural intervention.

The five mistakes below have been observed repeatedly across greenhouse projects of different scales over the years. What they share is that each one can be spotted at the start of a project — and prevented.

1. Starting the project without a site and local climate analysis

A door positioned without knowing the prevailing wind direction will increase heat loss significantly during winter. A wrongly oriented façade can turn a few hours of morning shade into a full day of light deficit. A flat site with no drainage assessment will collect water around the foundations after heavy rainfall.

A site analysis is not complete without an on-site visit and at least two seasons of meteorological data. The number of frost days in the region, wind speed records, and irrigation water quality tests are all core components of this phase. Projects that skip it tend to face unexpected costs during their first winter or first heavy rain event.

In addition, the planning status and agricultural land classification of the plot should be confirmed at this stage. A zoning issue discovered after construction has begun can bring a project to a complete halt.

2. Choosing the greenhouse type based on price, not on the crop

The difference between a plastic tunnel and a Venlo glass greenhouse is not only a matter of initial investment. What makes a structure right is the crop grown inside it, the level of climate control required, and the intended operational lifespan.

For long-season crops such as tomatoes, peppers, or cucumbers, keeping night-time and daytime temperatures within a tight range directly affects yield. In those conditions, a plastic tunnel may look cheaper in the short term, but energy losses and product risk can shift the balance over time. For seasonal crops or seedling production, on the other hand, a well-built plastic tunnel is often the most appropriate choice.

Start with the right question: Instead of asking "What can I build with this budget?", ask "What climate conditions does this crop require?" The type of greenhouse should follow from the answer to that question.

3. Irrigation and fertigation infrastructure added to the project as an afterthought

Deciding on the irrigation system after the greenhouse structure is finished is a serious planning error. The routing of main supply lines, the location of the pump room, and the position of the filtration station are all decisions that need to be settled before the foundations are laid. They directly affect the structural design.

Irrigation infrastructure added retroactively creates both technical and practical problems. Finished floors have to be broken up, or the system has to be fed from the main line through improvised connections. Retrofitting costs two to three times as much as planning from the outset.

Irrigation water quality also needs to be assessed at the very start of the project. High lime or salt content in the water source directly affects filtration and fertigation equipment selection. A system chosen without this information almost always requires revision later.

4. Operating costs are left out of the investment plan

Limiting the investment calculation to construction costs alone is a common but dangerous habit. Projects launched without a plan for first-year operating capital often run into cash flow difficulties before reaching their first harvest.

Monthly operating costs in a greenhouse project include the following:

  • Heating and energy (electricity, natural gas or biomass)
  • Irrigation water supply and fertigation nutrients
  • Seedling, seed or rootstock costs
  • Labour for pruning, harvesting and crop protection
  • Maintenance, consumables and insurance

The seasonal total of these items defines the outgoings that must be covered before any revenue is collected. A feasibility study is not complete without these figures. A realistic payback estimate requires construction cost, operating cost, and revenue projections to be considered together.

5. Starting the project without specialist input

A greenhouse project spans multiple areas of expertise: structural engineering, irrigation and fertigation, climate control, agronomy, and market planning. Projects launched without at least one feasibility consultation with an experienced professional in these fields are exposed to unforeseen problems as they progress.

The cost of a consultancy engagement is very small compared to the first major irreversible mistake. A structural inadequacy discovered after construction, a climate system chosen for the wrong crop, or an ill-suited irrigation plan — each of these can result in additional costs well above the original consultancy fee.

Conclusion: Mistakes are prevented at the start of the project

What all five of these mistakes have in common is that they occur at the beginning of a project — and all of them can be prevented by asking the right questions. Once construction has started, finding solutions becomes both more expensive and more limited.
